Karna Swasthya: The Ayurvedic View of Ear Health

In Ayurveda, ear care isn't an afterthought — it's tied to Vata balance and overall wellbeing. Here's what the classical texts say about keeping your ears comfortable, naturally.

Ayurveda treats the ears as a Vata-dominant zone — sensitive to dryness, cold, and imbalance elsewhere in the body. Classical texts describe Karna Purana (ear oiling) as a routine practice, not a treatment reserved for problems, meant to keep the passage supple and comfortable over time.

Herbs traditionally associated with ear wellness are chosen for their gentle, non-irritating nature — nothing sharp, nothing synthetic. The idea is consistency: small, regular care rather than occasional intervention.

This is also the thinking behind Ear Veda — a formula built on that same principle of steady, herb-led support rather than a one-time fix. As with any wellness routine, results build gradually and vary from person to person.

Piles, the Ayurvedic Way: Causes & Natural Comfort

Ayurveda links piles closely to digestion and daily habits. A look at what classical texts say about causes, and the gentle, herb-based approach to comfort.

In Ayurveda, Arsha (piles) is closely tied to Agni — digestive fire. When digestion is sluggish or irregular, waste lingers longer than it should, and that, over time, is seen as a root contributor to discomfort in the region.

Classical management leans on a small set of trusted herbs — Haritaki, Bibhitaki, and Amlaki (together, Triphala) chief among them — valued for supporting regular bowel movement and easing strain, alongside habits like adequate fibre and hydration.

Piles Veda draws on this same stack of herbs in liquid form, meant to be taken consistently as part of a broader routine — not as a substitute for medical care when symptoms are severe or persistent.

Triphala 101: The Three-Fruit Blend Every Cabinet Needs

Haritaki, Bibhitaki, and Amlaki — three fruits, one formula, and centuries of use. Here's why Triphala shows up in almost every classical Ayurvedic text.

Triphala literally means "three fruits" — Haritaki, Bibhitaki, and Amlaki — and it's one of the most referenced formulations in classical Ayurvedic literature. Each fruit is traditionally associated with a different dosha, which is part of why the combination is considered balanced rather than one-note.

Haritaki is linked to digestion and elimination, Bibhitaki to respiratory comfort, and Amlaki — rich in natural Vitamin C — to general vitality. Together, they're most commonly used for gut health and regularity.

It's simple, herb-only, and has stayed largely unchanged for centuries — part of why it's still a starting point for so many Ayurvedic formulas today, Piles Veda included.

5 Dinacharya Habits for Better Digestion

Dinacharya — Ayurveda's daily routine — puts as much weight on when you do things as what you do. Five simple habits worth borrowing from the old texts.

1. Wake with the sun. Ayurveda ties early rising to Vata time — a window considered better for clarity and a calmer digestive start to the day.

2. Warm water first. A glass of warm water on waking is a classical habit believed to gently kick-start Agni, the digestive fire.

3. Eat your largest meal at midday. When the sun is highest, digestion is traditionally considered strongest — lunch, not dinner, is meant to be the big meal.

4. Leave gaps between meals. Constant grazing is seen as taxing on Agni; a proper gap lets digestion finish before it starts again.

5. Wind down before bed. A quiet last hour — away from screens and heavy food — is considered essential to Ayurvedic sleep hygiene. None of this requires a product, just a shift in timing.

Meet the Herbs Behind Piles Veda

A closer look at the core herbs in Piles Veda — where they come from, and what classical Ayurveda credits them for.

Haritaki (Terminalia chebula) is often called the "king of herbs" in Ayurvedic texts, primarily valued for supporting healthy elimination and digestion.

Bibhitaki (Terminalia bellirica) is traditionally paired with Haritaki, credited with supporting respiratory comfort and, alongside its partner herbs, healthy digestion.

Amlaki (Indian gooseberry) is one of the richest natural sources of Vitamin C among Ayurvedic herbs, and is associated with general vitality.

Piles Veda combines these three with Aloe Vera, Yashtimadhu, Gokshura, Kutaj, and Arjuna — a stack chosen for working together rather than any single "hero" ingredient.

By Team ayushwellnes·12 Jun 2026·3 min read

How to Tell If a Supplement Is Truly Ayurvedic

"Ayurvedic" gets used loosely. Here's what to actually check on the label before you trust the claim.

A genuinely Ayurvedic product should list its herbs by name — not hide behind "proprietary blend." If you can't identify what's inside, that's the first flag.

Look for AYUSH or GMP-related certification marks, batch numbers, and a manufacturing address you can actually trace. These aren't decorative — they're what separates a real Ayurvedic manufacturer from a relabeled generic.

Finally, be wary of products promising overnight results. Classical Ayurveda has always framed herbs as supportive and cumulative, not instant — if the marketing promises a miracle, that's usually the tell that the formulation isn't.
